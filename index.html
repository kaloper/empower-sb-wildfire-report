How to read the results

How to interpret this dashboard

Higher values

Higher percentages mean that a larger share of respondents reported the activity, characteristic, or response shown. For rating questions, the chart or caption explains what a higher score represents.

FireWise comparisons

FireWise and non-FireWise values are survey comparisons. Differences should be interpreted with the sample size and statistical test rather than from bar height alone.

Statistical significance

A difference is statistically significant when the corresponding test has p < 0.05. Statistical significance does not by itself mean that a difference is large or practically important.

Sample sizes

The number answering can differ across questions because respondents did not necessarily answer every item. Use the reported n when a result provides one.

Defensible space2 / 8

Defensible space around the home

Defensible-space measures DefinitionReported actions that reduce combustible fuels around the home. Why it mattersReducing fuels can limit pathways for wildfire and embers to reach structures. How it was measuredSurvey responses to specific Zone 0, 1, and 2 actions and maintenance questions.
Key result

Respondents reported a mix of completed defensible-space actions across the three zones. Vegetation management received an average perceived-effectiveness score of 4.41 out of 5.

Defensible space reduces the fuels that can carry wildfire toward a structure. The results below pair the recommended activities for each zone with the specific actions reported by survey respondents.

Zone 0 · 0–5 feet

The area immediately next to the home

Zone 0 activities include removing dead plant material from roofs, gutters, decks, and the ground; removing live plants and other combustible materials; removing branches that enter the zone; and using non-combustible ground covers such as gravel, stone, or pavers.

Live plants removed within 5 feet90.9%n=77 answering
Non-combustible groundcover85.7%n=77 answering

The most common maintenance schedule was Monthly or more frequently (74.0%, n=77 answering).

Zone 1 · 5–30 feet

Lean, maintained vegetation and separated fuels

Zone 1 activities include regular removal of dead plant material, cutting grasses and weeds to three inches or lower, watering plants, trimming low or overhanging limbs, maintaining horizontal and vertical spacing, relocating large combustible items, and maintaining clear space around propane tanks and outbuildings.

Large shrub branches removed76.8%n=271 answering
Plants watered regularly88.6%n=271 answering

The most common maintenance schedule was Monthly or more frequently (66.7%, n=270 answering).

Zone 2 · 30–100 feet

Reduced fuels and continued vegetation spacing

Zone 2 activities include trimming annual grasses to four inches or lower, maintaining horizontal and vertical spacing, removing deep accumulations of dead plant material, creating bare-soil buffers around wood piles, and maintaining clear areas around propane tanks and outbuildings.

Shrub clumps horizontally separated40.0%n=60 answering
Annual grasses below 4 inches88.3%n=60 answering

The most common maintenance schedule was Monthly or more frequently (58.3%, n=60 answering).

What the defensible-space results mean

No single action is enough on its own. The three zones work together: Zone 0 limits combustible material next to the structure, Zone 1 interrupts the movement of flames through nearby vegetation, and Zone 2 reduces fuel continuity farther from the home. Regular maintenance is essential because vegetation and debris return over time.

What does this mean for Firewise Communities?

Defensible space is one of the most effective actions homeowners can take to reduce wildfire risk, but maintaining it requires ongoing effort. Firewise communities can use these results to encourage annual cleanup days, share defensible space resources, and celebrate residents who take action. When neighbors work together, the entire community becomes more resilient to wildfire.

Home hardening3 / 8

Reducing structural vulnerability

Home-hardening measures DefinitionMaintenance or building changes intended to reduce structural wildfire vulnerability. Why it mattersEmbers can ignite homes or enter through vulnerable building components. How it was measuredRespondents selected home-hardening or maintenance actions they reported completing.
Big takeaway

The three most frequently reported completed actions were Housekeeping (debris removal), Roof improvements, Signage / address numbers. Among those asked why improvements were not made, 9.2% cited financial means and 9.2% said they did not know it was a problem.

Debris removal and roof-related improvements were among the most frequently completed actions. Weather stripping around garage doors and window improvements were less common, showing that many homes may still have openings where wind-driven embers can enter.

  • Window improvements: 42.1% reported completing it.
  • Weather stripping (garage doors): 25.0% reported completing it.

Among respondents who explained why additional work had not been completed, financial constraints and limited awareness were recurring obstacles. 15.8% reported that the relevant work had already been completed. Because this answer applied to the home as a whole, it cannot be linked to one specific improvement.

What the home-hardening results mean

The survey includes both routine or relatively lower-cost maintenance actions and more substantial building upgrades. Routine actions include removing accumulated debris and maintaining or sealing existing components. More substantial structural improvements include work on windows, vents, roofing, siding, and major garage-door components.

Routine maintenance actions were generally more common than several structural improvements. Weather stripping around garage doors and window improvements were among the less commonly reported actions. Assistance programs may therefore have the greatest reach when they combine clear, home-specific recommendations with financial or technical support for more resource-intensive improvements such as window replacement, ember-resistant vent upgrades, roofing, siding, and major garage-door improvements.

What does this mean for Firewise Communities?

Home hardening is most effective when residents understand that every improvement helps, and that wildfire resilience doesn't stop at the property line. A hardened home is better protected when neighboring homes are also prepared. Firewise communities can encourage homeowners to start with simple, affordable upgrades, such as installing ember-resistant vents, replacing combustible mulch near the home, or maintaining roofs and gutters, while connecting residents with trusted guidance, home evaluation programs, funding opportunities, and resources for larger retrofits. By supporting residents at every stage, Firewise communities can help build safer, more resilient neighborhoods together.

Preparedness and readiness4 / 8

Actions households and communities have taken

Preparedness activities DefinitionActions completed before wildfire to improve household or community readiness. Why it mattersPlanning, drills, training, document protection, and assessments support faster emergency action. How it was measuredRespondents selected preparedness activities they reported completing.
Big takeaway

FireWise respondents reported higher completion percentages for nearly every listed preparedness activity. The largest gaps involved chipper events, wildfire education, training groups, and Home Ignition Zone assessments.

Backing up important documents and making a household emergency plan were among the most common preparedness steps. More resource-intensive or organized activities, such as evacuation drills, formal training, and Home Ignition Zone assessments, were much less common.

FireWise respondents reported higher participation in most activities, especially chipper events, wildfire education, training groups, and property assessments. In the table, a checkmark simply identifies activities for which the FireWise percentage was higher.

What does this mean for Firewise Communities?

Firewise respondents consistently reported higher participation in preparedness activities than non-Firewise respondents, particularly in chipper events, wildfire education, community training, and Home Ignition Zone (HIZ) assessments. Risk perception5 / 8

Experience, expectations, and neighborhood risk

Risk measures DefinitionReported wildfire experience and perceptions of future wildfire risk. Why it mattersRisk perception can influence motivation to prepare and communication needs. How it was measuredSurvey questions about prior wildfire experience and perceived likelihood or impact.
Big takeaway

57.7% reported having evacuated from home at least once. Perceived lifetime wildfire impact averaged 4.03 on the five-point likelihood scale.

Wildfire was not an abstract concern for many respondents: more than half had evacuated their home at least once, and substantial shares had seen an active fireline or experienced property loss nearby.

Respondents generally viewed wildfire as more likely to affect their home over their lifetime than during the next year. Most also viewed neighbors as valuable prevention partners, although nearby properties can increase shared risk when vegetation and structures are not maintained.

What does this mean for Firewise Communities?

Many residents already recognize wildfire as a significant and personal risk, with more than half reporting that they have evacuated due to wildfire. Firewise communities can build on this awareness by helping neighbors move from concern to action through education, preparedness activities, and community-based mitigation efforts. Turning awareness into action is a key step toward building long-term wildfire resilience.

Community connections6 / 8

Wildfire readiness is also social

Community preparedness measures DefinitionNeighborhood communication, social connection, and mutual assistance related to wildfire. Why it mattersNeighbors share evacuation routes, vegetation conditions, information, and assistance needs. How it was measuredSurvey questions about communication channels, events, conversations, and neighbor assistance.
Big takeaway

FireWise respondents were more likely to report neighborhood communication channels, social events, wildfire-preparedness groups, preparedness conversations, and helping or advising neighbors.

Everyday neighbor support was common, and many respondents reported communication through newsletters, social media, or neighborhood events. These existing relationships provide a foundation for sharing alerts, coordinating vegetation work, and checking on residents who may need assistance.

FireWise respondents were especially likely to report local preparedness groups, wildfire conversations with neighbors, and direct help or advice related to defensible space. The pattern suggests that organized community networks can make preparedness more visible and easier to act on.

01

Know how you will leave

Identify more than one route, decide where household members will meet, plan for anyone who may need assistance, and include pets or large animals. Keep vehicles fueled or charged and avoid waiting for the final moments to prepare.

  • Choose an out-of-area contact.
  • Practice loading people, animals, medications, and essential equipment.
  • Know the difference between an evacuation warning and an evacuation order.
02

Prepare what you will take

Keep a go bag where it can be reached quickly. Customize supplies for medications, mobility needs, children, animals, work equipment, and the length of time you may be away.

  • Water, food, flashlight, radio, chargers, and protective clothing.
  • Medication list, prescriptions, identification, and insurance records.
  • Digital backups of important documents and photographs.
03

Reduce fuels around the home

Begin at the structure and work outward. Remove leaves and needles from roofs and gutters, keep the first five feet as noncombustible as possible, maintain healthy and separated vegetation, and address ladder fuels that can carry fire into tree canopies.

  • Review Zones 0, 1, and 2 on the Defensible Space page.
  • Repeat maintenance throughout the year, not only once.
  • Coordinate with neighbors where vegetation and access are shared.
04

Harden vulnerable building components

Embers can enter through small openings or ignite combustible materials against the home. Prioritize roofs, vents, eaves, windows, siding, decks, fences, garage doors, and gaps where wind-driven embers can collect.

  • Use ember-resistant vents and maintain screens.
  • Seal garage-door gaps and inspect weather stripping.
  • Move combustible furniture and stored material away during high-risk periods.

Five actions to complete now

  1. Enroll every adult in SBC Alerts.
  2. Choose two evacuation routes and a meeting location.
  3. Assemble or update household go bags.
  4. Clear roofs, gutters, decks, and the first five feet around the home.
  5. Talk with at least one neighbor about alerts, assistance needs, and evacuation plans.

Firewise communities can help residents prepare by promoting emergency alerts, evacuation planning, home assessments, and ongoing mitigation efforts. When more neighbors take these steps together, the entire community is better prepared to respond to and recover from wildfire.

Central finding

Self-reported alignment with defensible-space guidance is high, but reported retrofit completion and specific defensible-space actions do not fully align with those perceptions. The gap suggests that awareness and confidence are not always translating into completed property-level mitigation.

Respondents generally rated wildfire mitigation, defensible space, and related protective strategies as effective. Where broad self-reported zone-alignment measures were available, reported alignment also tended to be favorable. In contrast, item-level results show that many specific vegetation-management actions and structural retrofits were completed by a smaller share of households.

This difference is important for program planning. Self-assessments can indicate confidence and awareness, while the action questions identify work that has actually been completed. Both should be considered, but they should not be treated as interchangeable measures of readiness.

How to interpret the tests

Tests are selected from a prespecified, board-only hypothesis configuration. Ordered measures with a directional hypothesis use a one-sided Mann-Whitney U test with the alternative FireWise > non-FireWise. Binary measures with a directional hypothesis use a one-sided Fisher exact test of whether the positive response is more common among FireWise respondents. Ordered and binary measures without a directional hypothesis remain two-sided. Unordered multi-category measures use a two-sided omnibus chi-square test of the full response table..

How to interpret this model

The coefficient is reported for one additional year that a FireWise community has been established. A positive coefficient means the selected outcome tends to be higher in longer-established communities. A negative coefficient means the selected outcome tends to be higher in newer communities. Statistical significance is evaluated at p < 0.05. The model is descriptive and should not be interpreted as causal.
